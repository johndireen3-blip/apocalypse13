Five readings are weighed so far. More will be added, each stated at its best before it is tested (Standard 29). Weighing an interpreter's proposal is not a verdict on the interpreter.

St. Irenaeus: the classic reading A Father or Doctor

St. Irenaeus of Lyon, writing around A.D. 180, gives the earliest full reading of the beast by a Father of the Church (Against Heresies V.25–30). He reads the beast from the sea as one man to come at the end, who receives all the power of the devil, sets himself up to be worshiped as God, and rises among ten kings into whom the Roman Empire is divided. He reads the beast from the earth as his armor-bearer, the false prophet. He will not guess the beast's name: the number, he says, was left hidden so that it would be recognized only when it is fulfilled (V.30.3).

He is weighed here as the classic reading because he applies the text to no one in the present, so his reading shows what a faithful reading of the marks looks like before any application is made. St. Hippolytus and St. Cyril of Jerusalem are drawn in where they add to him.

Fr. Charles Arminjon: the tradition gathered A theologian

Fr. Charles Arminjon (1824–1885), a priest of Chambéry in Savoy, was ordained in 1849 as a Jesuit, left the Society with its approval in 1859 to teach at the major seminary of Chambéry, and was named an apostolic missionary by Rome in 1863, after which he gave himself wholly to preaching. He preached nine conferences at the cathedral of Chambéry, published in 1881 as The End of the Present World and the Mysteries of the Future Life. St. Thérèse of Lisieux read it in 1887, at fourteen, and wrote that reading it "was one of the greatest graces of my life." In the Second Conference he gathers the Fathers and Doctors, above all Augustine, Jerome, Thomas, Bellarmine and Suárez, into one account. The Antichrist will be one man, possessed by Satan from his youth. He will demand worship as God, work false wonders, persecute the Church across the whole earth for forty-two months, and be struck down by Christ.

He reads one beast only, and gives the second beast's signs to the Antichrist himself. Like Irenaeus he names no one and will not decode 666. He warned that present calamities cannot prove the end is near (p. 13), yet from the signs of his own century he judged the Antichrist's nearness "more probable" than his remoteness, adding that centuries are little "compared with the years of eternity" (p. 21).

He is weighed here as the tradition's synthesis on the eve of the twentieth century, and because Fr. John reads with him. Page numbers are to the English translation by Susan Conroy and Peter McEnerny (Sophia Institute Press, 2008).

Fr. Vincent Miceli, S.J.: theology, not theology-fiction A theologian

Fr. Vincent P. Miceli, a Jesuit priest, wrote The Antichrist (1981, with the nihil obstat and imprimatur of the Archdiocese of New York) "in order to prevent the faithful from falling into erroneous predictions" by setting out the Church's teaching afresh (p. 16). With "all the Fathers and theologians" he holds that the Antichrist will be one individual person (p. 18), preceded by many lesser antichrists, his "types" and "precursors" (p. 33). He distinguishes the two beasts as the text does: the beast from the earth is the false prophet, the first beast's assistant (pp. 37–38).

His chief warning is against "theology-fiction": "the presentation of personal speculations as facts found in these prophecies" (p. 15). His rule is that "the intelligent and clear must be presented with discernment; the mysterious left for explanation by the events" (p. 21). He names no one, sets no date, and reads the irreligion of his own age as a warning, not a fulfillment.

He is weighed here as a modern Catholic theologian writing against the speculation of his day. Surveying the Middle Ages, he judges that holy men who believed the Antichrist had already come "allowed themselves to be deceived" (p. 85), and that "all chiliastic movements outlive the nonfulfillment of their prophecies" (p. 97). In chapter 8, "Modern Shadows," he names many modern figures and regimes as "types, precursors, shadows" of the Antichrist (p. 147), from Marx and Freud to Hitler and Stalin, and even Roosevelt and Churchill at Yalta and the Kennedy administration over the 1963 coup in South Vietnam (pp. 140–146), though he calls no one the Antichrist himself. The same chapter expects the Antichrist to rule from Jerusalem, with the State of Israel as his "power base" (p. 152), which this site weighs under Standard 28. Chapter 9 turns to "antichrists" within the Church, naming dissenting Catholic theologians and, without naming them, two Jesuit university presidents as "mini-antichrists" and "forerunners" (pp. 165–182), though he casts no one as the false prophet. In chapter 10 he gives, at last, his own view of the restrainer: "the strong Faith of the Church is what has restrained the coming of the Antichrist so far" (p. 222), and judges that "the portents of his nearness are evident everywhere," though "his name and his time are known only to God" (p. 219). In his last chapter he turns to Mary, the Woman of Revelation 12, whom he expects to "destroy" the Antichrist (p. 258). The book has been read in full; chapters 4–7 mostly report the Fathers, the medieval writers and Newman.

Peter Thiel: the world state A theologian

Peter Thiel, the technology investor and co-founder of PayPal and Palantir, is not a Catholic, but he has lectured widely on the Antichrist since 2024, including in Rome. His thesis, as he set it out on the record (Hoover Institution, Uncommon Knowledge, 2024): the Antichrist is a type that can be a person, a system or an ideology. In our age it will come to power by stirring fear of catastrophe (nuclear war, pandemics, artificial intelligence, climate) and offering "peace and safety" (1 Thessalonians 5:3) through a world government that halts science and technology. He sees runaway technology as the danger of Armageddon and a stifling world state as the danger of the Antichrist, and judges the second more likely.

Only what he has said or published on the record is weighed here. His private lectures are cited only as reported in the press, and his two essays in First Things are still to be read in full.

Fr. John: the American presidency Fr. John's proposal

Fr. John, a Catholic diocesan priest who founded and directs The Ephesus Challenge, reads the seven heads of the beast as seven world powers, counted from John's day, with the United States as the seventh. He proposes that the first beast of Revelation 13 is Donald J. Trump: the seventh head his first term, the eighth his return to power in the 47th presidency. He grades each mark possible or probable, never settled, and sets out the evidence against his reading beside the evidence for it. The Church has not taught this reading, and he does not say she has.

He makes his case on his own site. Here it is weighed by the same standards as every other reading, and he asks that it be tested.

Apocalypse 13

Every reading of Revelation 13, Fr. John's included, weighed in the same way: against the marks Scripture and the Church reveal, and against the Church's standards for reading Scripture. Verdicts are on arguments, never on persons.
