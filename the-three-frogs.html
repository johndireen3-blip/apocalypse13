Revelation 16 weighed

The Three Frogs

Three unclean spirits from three mouths, sent to gather the kings of the world: what each reading makes of them, and whether spirits can be read as three named men.

Home › The Marks Weighed › Revelation 16

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

The text
At the sixth bowl John sees "three unclean spirits like frogs" come from the mouth of the dragon, the mouth of the beast and the mouth of the false prophet. "These were demonic spirits who performed signs. They went out to the kings of the whole world to assemble them for the battle on the great day of God the almighty." Then Christ speaks: "Behold, I am coming like a thief. Blessed is the one who watches" (Revelation 16:13–16; Exodus 7:26–8:11; 1 Kings 22:21–23).
What the Church teaches
The Church has defined nothing about the three frogs. She teaches that Satan and the demons are fallen angels, pure spirits, whose power is real but "not infinite" (CCC 391–395), and that the final trial is a deception (CCC 675). Church teaching The commentators of the tradition read the frogs as spirits and as speech. A Father or Doctor
Authority
Scripture

What the text requires

Read in context (Standard 4), the passage says three things of the frogs. They come from a mouth: each is what the dragon, the beast or the false prophet speaks into the world. They perform signs. And they go out to the kings of the whole world, for battle. It also says plainly what they are: "demonic spirits." A reading that names human beings must first show why spirits may be read as persons at all (Standard 13), and then show all three things of each person. Because it sets living men beside demons, it is held most strictly to justice toward persons (Standard 10). And because the text places the frogs at the sixth bowl, a reading that sees them now is saying something about the time, and must not turn that into a date (Standard 5).

Verdict on the arguments

This is the reading on which Fr. John claims least, and that is to its credit. He grades all three names only possible, says that no sign has been shown for any of them, and says that none can rise higher until one is.

Its first difficulty comes before any evidence. The text calls the frogs "demonic spirits," and Bede and Haydock read them so: as devils, and as the lying speech that comes from the three mouths. Fr. John reports this fairly and answers it from Scripture: he does not say that any man is a demon, but that a lying spirit works through human mouths, "as it did through the prophets of Ahab" (1 Kings 22:22). That is a real argument, and it keeps the text's word "spirits" intact. What it shows is that such a thing can happen. It does not show that John meant three individual men, and no Father reads him so; in Ahab's court the one spirit spoke through four hundred prophets, not one. The step from spirits to three named persons is therefore permitted but not required by the text (Standard 13).

Tested by the text's three requirements, the names are uneven. Witkoff fits best, and Fr. John says so: he does go out to the kings of the world, in the false prophet's company. But he gathers them to negotiate, and the peace he helped make in Gaza is a real good. Miller's claim to the beast's mouth is the most literal, since he writes its words, but he gathers no kings. Musk's fit to the dragon's mouth is by likeness alone, and his public break with the president sits badly with three spirits that act as one. For none of the three is a sign shown.

The change from Mike Johnson to Stephen Miller deserves a plain word. A reading whose names move when an election moves has shown that it follows events (Standard 8). It counts for Fr. John that he recorded the change and his reasons in public, on his page and on Submitted for Review. It counts against the method that the first name was offered at all on grounds an election could remove.

On justice to persons: to say that a named man speaks for the mouth of the dragon is the gravest thing said on any of these sites, and Fr. John's page calls it that. The page weighs words and deeds, judges no soul, gives each man's defense, and invites hostility toward no one. Those safeguards are real. They do not remove the weight of the charge, and a grade of possible is the most that so grave a proposal, on this evidence, can bear (Standard 10).

The observation about Musk and the image of a frog is labeled as not evidence, and rightly. The frogs of Revelation are spirits, not pictures.

The tradition's reading remains the anchor: the frogs are the unclean speech of the dragon, the beast and the false prophet, and the passage ends with the Lord's word to the reader, "Blessed is the one who watches." The other readings weighed on this site do not take up the passage in the material reviewed.

These are verdicts on arguments, not on any person.
