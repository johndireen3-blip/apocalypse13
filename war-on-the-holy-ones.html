The mark
The beast is allowed to wage war against the holy ones and conquer them, and those who will not worship its image are put to death (Revelation 13:7, 15; 11:7; 12:17; Daniel 7:21–22, 25).

Mark 7 on The First Beast

What the mark requires

Read in context (Standard 4), the holy ones are "those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus" (Revelation 12:17), and the war is against them as such: it ends in their death for refusing to worship (Revelation 13:15). Conflict between the Church and a government over policy is common in history and is not yet the mark. A reading that claims it must show the faithful opposed or penalized for their faith or witness, and the war moving toward the conquest the text describes. Because the Church teaches that the final trial will "shake the faith of many believers" (CCC 675), a reading must also be careful not to call every hardship persecution (Standard 9).

The three readings

Peter Thiel Not in the material reviewed His Antichrist rules by fear and the promise of "peace and safety," not by open persecution of believers Not applicable —
Fr. John Yes The holy ones read as the Catholic Church, all Christians, and all who keep God's commandments; the war waged, as in Daniel 11:32–33, by deceit and by force. By deceit: "You won't have to vote anymore, my beautiful Christians" (2024); the faith joined to his person; Christians invited to side with him against their shepherds, as when his vice president asked whether the bishops were "worried about their bottom line" (2025). By force: the end of the policy that kept immigration enforcement away from churches, later checked by a court; a choir member deported and ICE waiting outside a Spanish Mass (2026); a religious sister arrested walking to Mass (2026); clergy refused permission to bring Communion to detainees until a court intervened, and a pastor shot with a pepper ball (2025). Works of mercy: Catholic Relief Services cut by up to half after the aid freeze, and the bishops' 50-year refugee partnership ended (2025). The shepherds: clashes with Pope Francis (2016, 2025) and Pope Leo XIV (2026), and the bishops' first special message since 2013. He adds a lesson from Nazi Germany, labeled an analogy and not evidence, and sets in advance the test that would move the mark to probable (his case) Enforcement turns on immigration status, not religion, and no one has been arrested for being a Christian; DHS says ICE does not raid churches. The administration presents itself as a defender of Christians, with a task force against anti-Christian bias and a Religious Liberty Commission on which bishops serve. Immigration and aid are prudential questions on which Catholics may differ; the aid freeze fell on every relief group. The Nazi analogy has real limits, which he states. Courts have protected the churches and restored Communion, and the Church speaks freely: the holy ones are not conquered 7 met; 4 partly met: pressure on the Church shown, war on the faith not shown; 10 met: the defense and the analogy's limits are stated; 28 met with care: the Shoah is not diminished; 23 met: the test is set in advance; 30: graded possible, foreshadowed

Verdict on the arguments

Fr. John documents a real and sustained conflict between the Church and the government over migrants and the works of mercy, in which clergy, a religious sister and parishioners have been touched by enforcement, the bishops have spoken with unusual unity, and two popes have been answered with contempt. His argument that the Church's ministry to the stranger is part of her witness to Jesus is sound Catholic teaching (Matthew 25:35; CCC 2241, 2447), and his reading of a war by deceit as well as force has a firm basis in Daniel 11:32 and CCC 675. But the conflict is over policy, not over the faith as such; those affected were not penalized for being Christians; the courts have repeatedly protected the Church; and drawing Christians to a ruler is hard to show without judging consciences, which this site does not do. His history of Nazi Germany is offered honestly as an analogy with its limits stated, and his test for the mark is set in advance. What his evidence shows is pressure on the Church's witness, not yet the war and conquest the text describes, and he grades it accordingly. St. Irenaeus reads the mark faithfully as a final persecution still to come. Thiel's reading does not take up this mark.

These are verdicts on arguments, not on any person.
