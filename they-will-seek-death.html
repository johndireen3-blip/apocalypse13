Revelation 9 weighed

They Will Seek Death and Not Find It

People who long to die, and death escapes them. Fr. John asks whether the promise of a deathless life made by technology could become that state, and whether it belongs beside the false prophet. The question is weighed here like any other.

The text
At the fifth trumpet the shaft of the abyss is opened and locusts come from its smoke. They may harm only those without the seal of God on their foreheads, and may not kill them, only torment them for five months. In that time these people "will seek death but will not find it"; they long to die, and death escapes them (Revelation 9:1–6; Job 3:21; Jeremiah 8:3).
What the Church teaches
The Church has defined nothing about this verse. She teaches that death entered through sin and that Christ has transformed it, so that Christian death has a positive meaning (CCC 1008–1010), and that the Christian hope is the resurrection of the body, God's gift (CCC 997–1001). Life and health are goods to be cared for reasonably (CCC 2288). On December 10, 2025, Pope Leo XIV spoke of views that promise an immortality within this world through technology, called this the transhuman scenario, and asked: "Could death really be defeated by science?" and whether a life without death would also be a happy one (General Audience, December 10, 2025). He named no person. Church teaching

Scripture

What the text requires

Read in context (Standard 4), the verse has four features: a torment sent from the abyss by God's permission; a limit of five months; victims marked out as those without God's seal; and death that flees from people who seek it. A reading that applies the verse to a technology must account for each of these, and must say how it stands with the commentators who have read the verse before (Standard 2). The verse belongs to Revelation 9, not to Revelation 13; a reading that joins it to the false prophet must show the link from the text and not only assert it (Standard 16).

Verdict on the arguments

Fr. John's reading is a possible resonance and no more. It answers to something real: the Pope himself has asked whether a life without death, were science to give it, would be a happy one, and that is close to the thought that such a life could become one a person cannot leave. The verse describes that state. But the reading has no support in the tradition and little in the details of the text. The commentators read the verse of penance, of the weight of affliction, or of the life to come. The text speaks of a plague of fixed length, sent on those without God's seal, and of death that flees unsought. And the thing itself does not exist: the reading explains an aspiration, not an event.

Oecumenius is its nearest neighbor, since he too sees people who suffer and cannot die, but he sets them after the resurrection.

The link to the false prophet is weaker still. It rests on an inference from 13:15 and on a single remark, more than four years old, which its speaker's circle called a joke. Hoping that medicine will lengthen life is not transhumanism, and nothing shows him funding, building or preaching a project to abolish death. As evidence about a man, the remark carries almost no weight; it is fairly recorded as a thing said, with its qualifiers and its walk-back, and left there.

What would change the standing: a promise of life without death preached as a salvation and joined to allegiance to a ruler, or the hand of the man proposed in such a project. Neither has been seen.
