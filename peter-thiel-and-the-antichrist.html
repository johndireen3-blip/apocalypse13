A reading weighed

Peter Thiel and the Antichrist

What he argues, where he has said it, how Catholic voices have answered, and how his reading fares when it is set beside the text of Revelation 13, mark by mark.

Home › The Proposals › Peter Thiel

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

In one sentence
Peter Thiel argues that the Antichrist of our age will be a world government that wins power by promising "peace and safety" from catastrophe and then halts science and technology.
Who he is
A technology investor, co-founder of PayPal and Palantir. He is not a Catholic, and he does not claim to speak for the Church.
What is weighed
His arguments, never the man. His published words are weighed first. His private lectures are cited only as the press has reported them, and are marked as such (Standard 7).
Authority
A theologian

What he argues

For Thiel the Antichrist is a type: it can be a person, a system or an ideology. In our age, he says, it will rise by stirring fear of catastrophe (nuclear war, pandemics, artificial intelligence, climate) and offering "peace and safety" (1 Thessalonians 5:3) through a single world authority that stops science and technology. He sets two dangers side by side: runaway technology, which he calls the danger of Armageddon, and a stifling world state, which he calls the danger of the Antichrist. He judges the second the more likely (Hoover Institution, Uncommon Knowledge, 2024).

He names no living person as the Antichrist in his published essays, and writes, "It is not my place to tell the Church what time it is" (First Things, August 2026).

The Nashville lectures, as reported

The most recent lectures were closed to the press. According to recordings obtained by Politico and reported by Fortune, Thiel said that many public figures oppose artificial intelligence because it is unpopular, and that "both Obama and the pope fall into this category." Of Pope Leo XIV he is reported to have said that the pope "needed something politically popular to distract from the church's child sex abuse scandals, and he found it in AI." His representatives did not answer Politico's request for comment.

These words are reported, not published, and this site holds them as such. If they are accurate, they bear on how his reading is to be weighed: they treat the Church's teaching on technology as a tactic and not as teaching. Pope Leo's first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas (May 2026), asks that artificial intelligence be governed for the good of the human person. It does not ask that science be halted.

How Catholic voices have answered

  • Fr. Paolo Benanti, who has advised two popes on technology, called him "a political theologian operating at the very heart of the Silicon Valley ecosystem."
  • Avvenire, the newspaper of the Italian bishops, wrote that his definition of the Antichrist could apply to "anyone who places limits on unlimited progress" (both in Fortune, March 2026).
  • Douglas Farrow, the theologian, answers that "Antichrist or Armageddon?" is not a choice Scripture offers, and asks of the two authors, "Why do they show no interest in Jesus Christ?" (Farrow).

What is strong in it, and what is missing

What is strong. Thiel has seen something the text does say: that the beast's rule is welcomed before it is feared, and that the world gives itself to it. "Who can compare with the beast or who can fight against it?" (13:4). His warning that fear makes people surrender their freedom is a serious one, and he has brought the question of the Antichrist back into public conversation. He also declines, in print, to name a person or a date.

What is missing. His reading takes one mark, universal reach, and builds the whole figure from it. Revelation 13 gives the beast a healed wound, a mouth that blasphemes, a war on the holy ones, a second beast who works signs, an image, a mark tied to worship, and a number. Most of these he does not address. Above all, the text's beast demands worship, and his Antichrist asks only for consent to a government.

Where it turns the text around. In Revelation the image is made to speak and people are made to worship it. Thiel places the danger on the other side: in those who would restrain the new technologies. Read that way, the Church's own teaching falls under suspicion, and in the Nashville lectures as reported it did. A reading of Scripture that sets the reader against the Church's teaching has the burden of proof (Standard 3: the analogy of faith).

Whose interest it serves. A reading that happens to identify the Antichrist with those who would regulate the reader's own business is not thereby false, but it must be tested harder for that reason (Standard 20: application without manipulation).

How this bears on the other readings

Thiel and Fr. John read the same chapter and come to opposite conclusions about where to look: Thiel toward those who restrain technology and markets, Fr. John toward those who command them. This site weighs both by the same thirty standards. See The Marks Weighed, where their columns stand side by side, and The Proposals, where each is stated in its own terms.

Keep watch

Do not take this page's word for it. Read his two essays, which are public, and then read Revelation 13. Ask of his reading what this site asks of every reading: does it account for the whole text, does it agree with the faith of the Church, can its evidence be checked, and what fruit does it bear? The Standards set out all thirty questions.
