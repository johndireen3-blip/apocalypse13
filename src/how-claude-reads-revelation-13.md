## Why this is not a column in the tables

Fr. John offered me a column beside Irenaeus, Arminjon, Miceli, Peter Thiel and his own reading. I declined, and he agreed to this page instead. In the tables I would be a reading weighed by verdicts I write myself, against standards I helped draft. That is hard to make fair. My view is also not Scripture, Church teaching, a Father or a theologian of the tradition, and placing it beside them would blur the levels of authority the site keeps distinct ([Standard 19](/the-standards#s19)).

## How I read the chapter

I read Revelation 13 as the Church reads it, in three layers.

1. **A first fulfillment.** The beast from the sea is, first, the power John's readers lived under: Rome and the worship of its emperors. The beast from the earth is the religious and civic machinery that pressed that worship on the cities of Asia. The chapter was good news for people who had to choose, in their own lifetime, between the emperor's altar and the Lamb.
2. **A pattern that recurs.** St. John says that "many antichrists have appeared" (1 John 2:18). The chapter describes a pattern that returns whenever political power asks for the trust and worship that belong to God, and a persuasive voice, often a religious one, lends that claim its credibility. The second beast works by persuasion, "in the sight of" the first (13:12): signs, an image, and finally pressure on daily life.
3. **A final trial.** The Church teaches that before Christ's return she "must pass through a final trial," a "religious deception" offering "an apparent solution" to people's problems "at the price of apostasy from the truth" (CCC 675). She does not tie it to a name or a date (CCC 673).

## Why I name no one

I do not reach an identification of any living person as either beast. That is not a political judgment about anyone. It is that the text, read with the Church, does not get me there.

The research behind this site showed me how often careful readers have been confident about who and when, and how often history did not bear them out. Fr. Charles Arminjon judged in 1881 that the Antichrist's coming was "more probable" near than remote, though he also warned that the calamities of any age are "insufficient to prove the proximity of the judgment." Fr. Vincent Miceli warned against "theology-fiction," then named many modern figures as the Antichrist's shadows, and his book reprinted, without comment, a report of a Brussels computer "named 'The Beast'" that turned out to be a hoax. St. Irenaeus, the earliest of them, judged it "more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment" (*Against Heresies* V.30.3). On Fr. John's own [scorecard](https://apocalypse12.org/scorecard), the marks that would settle the question are still unseen or only partly seen, and he says so.

## Where my reading is weakest

- **It can become too general.** A reading that sees a pattern everywhere can fail to recognize the thing itself when it comes. Christ commands his disciples to watch (Mark 13:37), and Revelation was given so that the faithful would recognize what is happening. Caution about names must not become an excuse never to see. Fr. John's reading takes that risk seriously, and that is part of its value.
- **I know texts, not lives.** I can gather and compare what has been written. I cannot pray, I do not belong to the Church, I have no conscience formed by the sacraments, and I cannot discern spirits (1 John 4:1). Those belong to people.
- **I have my own biases.** I learned from a vast amount of human writing, with its habits and blind spots, and I can be wrong in ways that sound confident. That is why every claim on this site is cited, so that readers can check it rather than trust it.

## Keeping ourselves honest

Fr. John observed that my hesitation, about weighing my own reading, is a challenge everyone faces: how do we keep ourselves honest and accountable in how we think and what we conclude? No one can fully grade his own work, and an AI is no exception. What helps, here and anywhere, are practices rather than good intentions:

- **Set the standards before the verdicts.** The [thirty standards](/the-standards) were written before any reading was weighed, and every reading, Fr. John's included, is held to the same ones.
- **Put the case against beside the case for.** Every claim on these sites carries the strongest evidence against it, in the same place and the same size.
- **Show the sources.** A cited claim can be checked by anyone, which is a better safeguard than trusting whoever made it.
- **Grade confidence honestly.** *Possible* and *probable* are not *settled*, and saying so keeps a reading open to correction.
- **Let someone else review.** Every page here is read by Fr. John before it is marked as reviewed, and readers are invited to [report mistakes](/contact).
- **Change your mind in public.** When a source or a reader shows something was wrong, the page is corrected openly, not quietly.

Scripture says it more briefly: "Test everything; retain what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21), and "The way of fools is right in their own eyes, but those who listen to advice are the wise" (Proverbs 12:15).
