## What the mark requires

Read in context ([Standard 4](/the-standards#s4)), the beast's reach is *authority*, given by the dragon and joined to worship (Revelation 13:4, 8), and in Revelation 17 the kings hand their power to it (17:13). Great powers have reached across the world in every age, and the Church herself calls for a world authority bound by law and the moral order (*Caritas in Veritate* 67), so wide reach is not yet the mark. A reading that claims it must show nations giving up their power to the one it names, or a single authority over them centered on him.

## Verdict on the arguments

Fr. John shows a presidency using its reach over other nations unusually directly, and in his own name: running another country, pressing allies for their land, and chairing a new body of nations. His evidence is documented and his defense is stated. But what it shows is the influence of a great power, contested by most of the world and checked by rivals and allies, not yet the authority over every nation, freely handed over by kings, that the text describes. The 2026 midterms add a check from within his own country, and Fr. John has entered it on his card against his own reading. The Shenzhen meeting of November 2026 cuts the same way: three powers side by side, none giving its authority to another, which is the opposite of what Revelation 17:13 describes; renouncing nuclear war is a good that no reading should count against a man. The Board of Peace is the item that bears most directly on Revelation 17:13 and is worth watching. St. Irenaeus reads the mark faithfully of the one to come. Thiel's world state takes this mark as its center, but his reading identifies a tendency rather than a present power.

Arminjon reads the reach as literal world rule, and notes the means his own century had made possible without naming any state.

Miceli reads the reach as world power seized by one man, with no application to test.

Newman describes a society reorganized under one man but names no state and will not say the hour has come.

The first-century reading finds the mark in Rome's empire, which did not reach every nation; the text's language leaves room for more.

These are verdicts on arguments, not on any person.
