## What the mark requires

A reading that claims mark 2 must read the beast as Scripture explains it: a power over nations (Daniel 7:17), first fulfilled in Rome ([Standard 15](/the-standards#s15)), with its heads and horns read as Revelation itself explains them (Revelation 17:9–12; [Standard 16](/the-standards#s16)).

## Verdict on the arguments

St. Irenaeus meets every standard that applies. Thiel's world-state reading fits Daniel's sense of a kingdom, but does not engage the text's details. Fr. John's reading gives a full count anchored in John's own day, and its account of the seventh and eighth heads follows Revelation 17:8–11 closely. It rests on a minority count of the heads, an eleven-century gap, and a test for what qualifies as a head that depends on judgment. Its answer to the Cleveland comparison depends on mark 3.

Arminjon counts ten heads rather than seven, so his reading offers no account of the seven heads to set beside the others.

Miceli reads the seven heads as the emperors of John's own day, the first fulfillment, and says nothing of a later one.

Newman reads the beast as Daniel's fourth, the Roman Empire still living in its successor kingdoms, and is candid that the ten kings cannot be counted in history. That candor is the model for every later reading of the heads and horns.

The first-century reading takes the heads as Rome's emperors and declines to count them. A reading that counts them differently, as Fr. John's does, must first say why the emperors are not enough ([Standard 15](/the-standards#s15)). Since November 15, 2026, he does: he accepts the emperors as the first fulfillment, and argues that no count of them has fit "five have fallen, one is," that Daniel's beasts are kingdoms, and that Rome fell without the end coming. That meets the standard in form. It shows that a further fulfillment may be looked for; as he says himself, it "does not show that mine is it."
