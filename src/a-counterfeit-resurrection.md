## What the mark requires

Read in context ([Standard 4](/the-standards#s4)), the mark has three parts: a wound that seemed mortal, a healing, and a world that follows in fascination and then worships (Revelation 13:3–4, 12). Because it counterfeits the Resurrection, a reading that claims it should show more than a setback overcome.

## Verdict on the arguments

Fr. John's reading gives the mark's first two parts a concrete fulfillment and a clear sequence, and it argues the third from documented reactions. Its weight rests on whether the wound was "mortal" in more than a political sense, and on whether fascination turns into worship, which is the test of mark 6. The 2026 midterms weigh against the third part: a nation that votes against him at the first chance is not asking "who can fight against it?" Fr. John records the vote on his own card and draws no conclusion from it either way, which is fair. St. Irenaeus and St. Hippolytus read the mark faithfully, but leave the wound unexplained or read it of an empire. Thiel's material does not take up this mark.

Arminjon reads the wound literally, as a feigned death and rising of one man, a reading the text allows but does not require.

Miceli, following Newman, reads the healed head as the Roman empire revived by the Antichrist, a reading of an empire's return rather than a man's.

Newman reads the revival of the empire, not of a man; Miceli follows him.

The first-century reading explains the wound by a legend John's readers knew, Nero's return. It shows that the image had a meaning before any modern comeback, and that a "healed wound" could be said of a ruler's expected return to power. Fr. John now takes this up on his own card: he reads the Nero legend as the image's first meaning, left unfulfilled, and grants that a pattern loose enough to fit it "will fit many returns to power."
