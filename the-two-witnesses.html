Revelation 11 weighed

The Two Witnesses

Two prophets in sackcloth, killed by the beast and raised by God: what each reading makes of them, and whether a living man can be named as one.

Home › The Marks Weighed › Revelation 11

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

The text
God commissions "my two witnesses" to prophesy for 1,260 days in sackcloth. Fire comes from their mouths; they can shut the sky and turn water to blood. "When they have finished their testimony," the beast from the abyss kills them; their bodies lie in the street of the great city for three and a half days; then the breath of life from God enters them and they go up to heaven in a cloud (Revelation 11:3–12; Zechariah 4; Malachi 3:23; Matthew 17:10–13).
What the Church teaches
The Catechism does not name the witnesses. It teaches that the Messiah's coming awaits his recognition by "all Israel" (CCC 674; Romans 11:25–26), and that the Church will follow her Lord through a final trial "in his death and Resurrection" (CCC 675, 677). Church teaching That Elijah will come before the day of the Lord rests on Scripture and on the common expectation of the Fathers. A Father or Doctor
Authority
Scripture

What the text requires

Read in context (Standard 4), the passage gives four things by which any identification can be tested: two who testify together, for a measured time, in sackcloth; signs like those of Elijah and Moses; death at the hands of the beast, with their bodies exposed before the world; and life given back by God. The tradition is not of one mind about who they are, so there is freedom here, but it is freedom within what the Fathers have handed on (Standard 17). A reading that names a living man must also meet two cautions: it may not set a time (Standard 5), and since the text ends in the witnesses' death, it must not lead anyone to expect or to wish a man's death (Standard 10).

Verdict on the arguments

On Elijah, Fr. John stands with the tradition. Scripture promises him by name, the Lord confirms that "Elijah will indeed come," and Arminjon and Miceli both expect him. Grading that probable claims no more than the Fathers do. His further step, that Elijah's standing is "partly seen" in John the Baptist, has the Lord's own words for it (Matthew 17:12), but those words concern the first coming. The witnesses of Revelation 11 work signs, are killed by the beast and lie unburied in the great city, and John did none of these. The reading is permitted as a figure; as a fulfillment of Revelation 11 it is not shown.

On Pope Leo XIV the reading is new. It has real roots: Cyprian and Bede read the witnesses as the Church, and the Peter-and-Paul reading already places Peter there. And what it claims as fact is true and checkable: this Pope has spoken publicly, in Christ's name, against the works of the man Fr. John proposes as the first beast, and has been answered by him. But that is one of the text's four tests. The other three are unmet, as Fr. John says himself, and the reading rests on a prior identification that is itself graded only possible or probable. A proposal that meets one test of four, on a foundation not yet settled, is better described as possible than probable (Standard 9). Fr. John has kept probable as his own judgment and has said plainly what it does not rest on; this page records the difference and leaves the grade his.

The suggestion that the two might testify at different times he holds "as a bare possibility," and the text weighs against it: one street, the same three and a half days, the same onlookers, one rising (11:8–12).

His two observations, on the two beginnings in 2025 and on Fatima's hundredth year, are labeled as not evidence, counted from nothing and pointed at no date. Held that way they break no standard. They would break Standard 5 and Standard 24 the moment anyone counted forward from them.

The safeguards are the reading's strongest feature. It sets no time, forbids its readers to expect anyone's death, and ends in prayer for the Pope. Arminjon and Miceli give the tradition's answer for the second witness, Enoch, with the freedom the Fathers leave. Thiel's reading does not take up the passage.

The first-century reading, in the Bible Fr. John quotes, judges that the witnesses are "most probably" the universal Church and her martyrs, and lists Peter and Paul among the proposals. That supports the half of his reading that sees the Church in the second witness, and it weighs against naming any one man.

These are verdicts on arguments, not on any person.
