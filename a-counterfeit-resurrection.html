The mark
One of the beast's heads seemed mortally wounded, but the wound was healed, and the whole world followed after the beast (Revelation 13:3, 12, 14; 17:8).

Mark 3 on The First Beast

What the mark requires

Read in context (Standard 4), the mark has three parts: a wound that seemed mortal, a healing, and a world that follows in fascination and then worships (Revelation 13:3–4, 12). Because it counterfeits the Resurrection, a reading that claims it should show more than a setback overcome.

The five readings

Then, suddenly, he will rise" (The End of the Present World, p. 46) A literal wound in one man's body, where St. Hippolytus reads the empire; he does not say how the world's fascination follows 1, 2 met; 9 met (no one named)

Fr. John Yes The loss of office in 2020–21 and the chain of events that followed (January 6, a second impeachment, four indictments and a conviction) are the wound; the re-election of November 2024 and the return to power in January 2025 are the healing. The world's fascination: TIME's 2024 Person of the Year essay, world leaders and business leaders aligning with him (his case) Whether a lost election seems a mortal wound is a judgment, and the text speaks of a wound "by the sword" (13:14). Others read the wound as the Butler shooting of July 13, 2024: nearer the sword, but not mortal, and with no loss of power. A return from ruin alone is not unique: Lula in Brazil returned to the presidency after prison. Fascination is not yet worship 4 partly met: wound and healing shown, fascination argued, worship still to be shown (mark 6); 7 met; 30: graded probable

Verdict on the arguments

Fr. John's reading gives the mark's first two parts a concrete fulfillment and a clear sequence, and it argues the third from documented reactions. Its weight rests on whether the wound was "mortal" in more than a political sense, and on whether fascination turns into worship, which is the test of mark 6. St. Irenaeus and St. Hippolytus read the mark faithfully, but leave the wound unexplained or read it of an empire. Thiel's material does not take up this mark.

Arminjon reads the wound literally, as a feigned death and rising of one man, a reading the text allows but does not require.

Miceli, following Newman, reads the healed head as the Roman empire revived by the Antichrist, a reading of an empire's return rather than a man's.
